How to Use Spaced Repetition & Active Recall to Ace Tech Interviews

Published July 11, 2026 By PrepFlow Team

Coding interview preparation is notoriously overwhelming. Software engineers spend hundreds of hours solving hundreds of LeetCode challenges, only to find themselves completely blanking out when asked to solve a problem they solved just three weeks ago.

The problem isn't intelligence or coding competence; it's a structural limitation of human memory known as the Forgetting Curve. First discovered by psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus, the forgetting curve shows that we forget up to 80% of newly learned information within 48 hours unless we actively review it.

To beat the forgetting curve and master the massive scope of technical interviews, you need a scientific approach: Active Recall combined with Spaced Repetition.

1. What is Active Recall?

Active recall is the process of actively testing your brain to retrieve information rather than passively reading a solution.

When you stare at a solved code template or read a blog post explaining how to detect a cycle in a linked list, you are practicing passive recognition. Your brain feels comfortable because it recognizes the pattern, giving you a false sense of mastery.

Active recall breaks this illusion. By forcing yourself to write the code from scratch, map out the algorithm on a whiteboard, or explain the time complexity without looking at the answer, you strengthen the neural pathways associated with that concept.

2. The Spaced Repetition Method (The SM-2 Algorithm)

Active recall is most powerful when scheduled systematically. Spaced repetition spaces out your reviews over increasing intervals. If you review a topic right before you are about to forget it, the cognitive effort required to recall it cements it deeply into your long-term memory.

The gold standard for spaced repetition is the SM-2 algorithm, which computes the ideal next review date using a dynamic multiplication factor (the E-Factor) based on your confidence level:

  • Confidence score of 5 (Perfect recall): The interval increases significantly (e.g., review in 21 days).
  • Confidence score of 3 (Recalled with difficulty): The interval increases slightly (e.g., review in 4 days).
  • Confidence score of 1 or 2 (Failed recall): The interval resets back to 1 day to build reinforcement.

3. Step-by-Step Plan to Structure Your Prep

  1. Map Your Core Subjects: Divide your preparation into distinct categories: Arrays, Graphs, Dynamic Programming, System Design, and Behavioral Stories.
  2. Build Your Question Cards: For every problem you solve, create a question card listing the problem statement and constraints. Leave the solution hidden.
  3. Practice Active Retrieval: When reviewing, do not look at your previous solution. Try to explain the algorithm out loud or write the pseudocode.
  4. Log Your Recall Rating: Record how easily you retrieved the solution. Let the scheduling system set the next revision day.

Conclusion

Stop wasting hours re-solving random problems. Shift from passive reading to active, spaced reviews. By incorporating active recall and spaced repetition into your daily study routing, you will retain complex code patterns, solve coding interview questions faster, and approach your interviews with confidence.
